# Ethical theories: Kant, utilitarianism, virtue ethics

> **Why it matters:** Exams ask which thinker is linked to which theory, the famous phrases ("greatest happiness of the greatest number", "categorical imperative", "golden mean"), and whether a theory judges actions by consequences, duty or character.

## Three main families of normative ethics 🔥
| Family | Judges an act by … | Key thinkers | Key idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consequentialism / teleological ethics** (পরিণামবাদ) | Its **results** | **Jeremy Bentham, John Stuart Mill** (utilitarianism) | The right act produces the most good |
| **Deontology / duty ethics** (কর্তব্যবাদ) | Whether it follows a **duty or rule** | **Immanuel Kant** | Some acts are right or wrong in themselves |
| **Virtue ethics** (সদ্গুণ নীতিবিদ্যা) | The **character** of the person | **Aristotle** (also Plato, Confucius) | Be a good person; right acts follow |

"Deontology" comes from the Greek **"deon"** = duty. "Teleological" comes from **"telos"** = end or goal.

## Kant's duty ethics 🔥
- **Immanuel Kant** (1724–1804), German philosopher.
- Main ethical book: ***Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*** (1785); also ***Critique of Practical Reason*** (1788). His ***Critique of Pure Reason*** (1781) is on knowledge.
- **Good will** is the only thing good **without qualification**.
- An act has moral worth only if done **from duty**, not from desire or for results.
- **Hypothetical imperative:** "If you want X, do Y" (conditional).
- **Categorical imperative (নিঃশর্ত আদেশ):** an unconditional moral command:
  1. **Universal law formula:** "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a **universal law**."
  2. **Humanity formula:** treat humanity, in yourself or others, **always as an end and never merely as a means**.
- Result: lying is always wrong, even to get a good result.
- Famous line: "Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe … **the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me**."

## Utilitarianism (উপযোগবাদ) 🔥
| | **Jeremy Bentham** (1748–1832) | **John Stuart Mill** (1806–1873) |
|---|---|---|
| Role | **Founder** of modern utilitarianism | Refined and defended it |
| Main book | ***An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*** (1789) | ***Utilitarianism*** (1861 as essays, 1863 as a book); also ***On Liberty*** (1859) |
| Principle | **"The greatest happiness of the greatest number"** is the measure of right and wrong | Same principle (greatest happiness principle) |
| Pleasure | **Quantitative**: pleasures differ only in amount; measured by the **hedonic (felicific) calculus** | **Qualitative**: higher (intellectual) pleasures are better than lower (bodily) ones |
| Famous line | "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, **pain and pleasure**." | "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be **Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied**." |

- **Act utilitarianism:** judge each act by its results. **Rule utilitarianism:** follow rules that generally produce the most happiness.
- Criticism: it can justify harming a minority if the majority gains.

## Virtue ethics (সদ্গুণ নীতি) 🔥
- **Aristotle** (384–322 BC), ***Nicomachean Ethics***.
- The goal of life is **eudaimonia**: happiness, flourishing, living well.
- Virtue is a **habit**: "we become just by doing just acts".
- **Golden mean (মধ্যপন্থা):** a virtue is the middle between two vices:

| Deficiency (vice) | **Virtue (mean)** | Excess (vice) |
|---|---|---|
| Cowardice | **Courage** | Rashness |
| Meanness | **Generosity** | Extravagance (wastefulness) |
| Mock modesty (understatement) | **Truthfulness** | Boastfulness |

- Aristotle divided virtues into **intellectual** (learned by teaching) and **moral** (learned by habit).
- Virtue ethics was revived in the 20th century by **G. E. M. Anscombe** ("Modern Moral Philosophy", 1958) and **Alasdair MacIntyre** (***After Virtue***, 1981).

## Other theories in brief
| Theory | Thinker | Key idea |
|---|---|---|
| Hedonism | **Epicurus** | Pleasure (absence of pain) is the highest good |
| Social contract | **Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau** | Morality and the state come from an agreement among people |
| Justice as fairness | **John Rawls**, *A Theory of Justice* (1971) | Choose principles behind a **"veil of ignorance"** |
| Ethical egoism | — | Act in your own long-term interest |
| Divine command | — | Right is what God commands |

## Quick revision
- Consequences → utilitarianism; duty → Kant; character → virtue ethics.
- Bentham: "greatest happiness of the greatest number"; quantitative pleasure; hedonic calculus.
- Mill: qualitative pleasure; "Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied"; *Utilitarianism*, *On Liberty*.
- Kant: good will, duty, categorical imperative, "end, never merely a means".
- Aristotle: eudaimonia, golden mean, *Nicomachean Ethics*.
- Courage is the mean between cowardice and rashness.
- Rawls: veil of ignorance, *A Theory of Justice* (1971).

## Practice MCQ
**1.** "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" is the principle of:
(a) Kant (b) Jeremy Bentham (c) Aristotle (d) Plato

**2.** The "categorical imperative" is associated with:
(a) John Stuart Mill (b) Epicurus (c) Immanuel Kant (d) Thomas Hobbes

**3.** Which theory judges an action mainly by its consequences?
(a) Utilitarianism (b) Kant's deontology (c) Virtue ethics (d) Divine command theory

**4.** According to Aristotle, courage is the mean between:
(a) wisdom and folly (b) pride and humility (c) generosity and meanness (d) cowardice and rashness

**5.** Who said, "It is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied"?
(a) Bentham (b) J. S. Mill (c) Kant (d) Rousseau

**6.** Kant's main ethical work is:
(a) Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (b) Nicomachean Ethics (c) Leviathan (d) The Republic

**7.** Virtue ethics is mainly concerned with:
(a) results of actions (b) the character of the person (c) rules made by the state (d) commands of the majority

**8.** Bentham's method of measuring pleasure and pain is called:
(a) the golden mean (b) the veil of ignorance (c) the categorical imperative (d) the hedonic (felicific) calculus

**9.** The "veil of ignorance" is an idea of:
(a) John Rawls (b) Aristotle (c) Bentham (d) Kant

## Answer key
| Q | Ans | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | b | Bentham founded utilitarianism on this principle. |
| 2 | c | Kant's unconditional moral command. |
| 3 | a | Utilitarianism is a consequentialist theory. |
| 4 | d | Aristotle's golden mean: courage lies between cowardice and rashness. |
| 5 | b | Mill, arguing that pleasures differ in quality. |
| 6 | a | Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785). |
| 7 | b | Virtue ethics focuses on being a good person. |
| 8 | d | Bentham's felicific or hedonic calculus. |
| 9 | a | Rawls, A Theory of Justice (1971). |
