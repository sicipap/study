# Literary terms: allegory, satire, figures of speech

> **Why it matters:** Exams ask definitions ("A story with a second, symbolic meaning is…"), famous examples (*The Pilgrim's Progress*, *Gulliver's Travels*), and "Identify the figure of speech" in a quoted line.

## Allegory and related forms 🔥
- **Allegory:** a story in which characters and events stand for **abstract ideas or a second meaning** (moral, religious or political).

| Work | Author | Type of allegory |
|---|---|---|
| ***The Pilgrim's Progress*** (1678) 🔥 | **John Bunyan** | Religious; hero **Christian** travels from the City of Destruction to the Celestial City |
| ***The Faerie Queene*** | **Edmund Spenser** | Moral and political; each knight = a virtue |
| ***Everyman*** (15th c.) | Anonymous | **Morality play** |
| ***Animal Farm*** (1945) | **George Orwell** | Political (Russian Revolution) |
| ***Absalom and Achitophel*** (1681) | **John Dryden** | Political satire in biblical disguise |
| ***The Crucible*** | Arthur Miller | Salem trials = McCarthyism |

| Related term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fable** | Short tale with animals, ending in a moral | **Aesop**'s fables |
| **Parable** | Short tale with a moral or spiritual lesson (human characters) | Parables of Jesus (the Prodigal Son) |
| **Symbol** | An object standing for an idea | Albatross in "The Ancient Mariner" |

## Satire 🔥
- **Satire:** literature that **ridicules** the follies and vices of people or society, to correct them. Tools: irony, sarcasm, parody, exaggeration.
- **Horatian satire:** gentle, amused (from Horace). **Juvenalian satire:** bitter, angry (from Juvenal).

| Work | Author | Target |
|---|---|---|
| ***Gulliver's Travels*** (1726) 🔥 | **Jonathan Swift** | Mankind and politics; **Lilliput** (tiny people), **Brobdingnag** (giants), **Laputa** (floating island of scientists), land of the **Houyhnhnms** (rational horses) and **Yahoos** (brutish humans) |
| "**A Modest Proposal**" (1729) | **Swift** | Irish poverty; ironically suggests eating babies |
| ***Mac Flecknoe***, ***Absalom and Achitophel*** | **Dryden** | Poet Shadwell / Whig politics |
| ***The Rape of the Lock***, ***The Dunciad*** | **Alexander Pope** | Fashionable society / dull writers |
| ***Hudibras*** | Samuel Butler | Puritans |
| ***Don Juan*** | **Lord Byron** | Society |
| ***Don Quixote*** | **Cervantes** | Chivalric romances |

| Related term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Parody** | Comic imitation of a particular work or style |
| **Burlesque** | Grotesque, comic treatment (high subject made low or low made high) |
| **Lampoon** | Harsh, personal satire on an individual |
| **Sarcasm** | Bitter, taunting remark |
| **Invective** | Direct abuse |

## Figures of speech 🔥🔥
| Figure | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simile** | Comparison using ***like / as*** | "**My love is like a red, red rose**" (Burns); "I wandered lonely **as** a cloud" (Wordsworth) |
| **Metaphor** | Implied comparison, no *like/as* | "**All the world's a stage**" (Shakespeare); "Life is a dream" |
| **Personification** | Giving human qualities to non-human things | "**Death lays his icy hand on kings**" (James Shirley) |
| **Apostrophe** | Addressing an absent person or a thing | "**Milton! thou shouldst be living at this hour**" (Wordsworth); "O Death, where is thy sting?" |
| **Hyperbole** | Deliberate exaggeration | "**Ten thousand saw I at a glance**" (Wordsworth); "I have told you a million times" |
| **Metonymy** | A related thing stands for the thing meant | "**The pen is mightier than the sword**" (pen = writing, sword = force); "The **Crown**" = the king |
| **Synecdoche** | A part for the whole, or whole for part | "**All hands** on deck"; "our daily **bread**" (= food) |
| **Oxymoron** | Two opposite words **side by side** | "**bitter-sweet**", "**open secret**", "**O loving hate!**" (Romeo and Juliet), "cruel kindness" |
| **Paradox** | A statement that seems absurd but hides a truth | "**The Child is father of the Man**" (Wordsworth); "More haste, less speed" |
| **Antithesis** | Contrasting ideas in balanced phrases | "**Man proposes, God disposes**"; "**To err is human, to forgive divine**" (Pope) |
| **Alliteration** | Repetition of the **same initial consonant sound** | "**P**eter **P**iper **p**icked a **p**eck…"; "The **f**air breeze blew, the white foam **f**lew, / The **f**urrow **f**ollowed **f**ree" (Coleridge) |
| **Assonance** | Repetition of **vowel sounds** | "Hear the mellow wedding bells" |
| **Onomatopoeia** | Word imitating a sound | **buzz, hiss, murmur, crash, cuckoo** |
| **Climax** | Ideas in rising order of importance | "**I came, I saw, I conquered.**" |
| **Anticlimax / bathos** | Sudden fall from the important to the trivial | "Here thou, great Anna! whom three realms obey, / Dost sometimes counsel take—and sometimes tea." (Pope) |
| **Euphemism** | Mild word for an unpleasant idea | "He **passed away**" (= died) |
| **Litotes** | Understatement by denying the opposite | "He is **no fool**"; "not bad" |
| **Transferred epithet** | Adjective moved from the proper noun to another | "a **sleepless night**" (the person, not the night, is sleepless) |
| **Irony** | Meaning opposite to the words / result opposite to expectation | Verbal, situational, **dramatic irony** (audience knows more than the character) |
| **Pun** | Play on two meanings of a word | "Ask for me tomorrow, and you shall find me a **grave** man" (Mercutio) |
| **Zeugma** | One word applied to two others in different senses | "She lost her hat and her temper" |
| **Rhetorical question** | Question asked for effect, no answer expected | "**If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?**" (Shelley) |

**Simile vs metaphor:** simile says *like/as*; metaphor says one thing *is* another.
**Oxymoron vs paradox:** oxymoron = two contradictory **words**; paradox = a contradictory **statement**.

## Other terms often asked
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Conceit** | Far-fetched, elaborate comparison; **metaphysical conceit** (Donne's compasses in "A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning") |
| **Catharsis** | Purging of **pity and fear** through tragedy (**Aristotle**, *Poetics*) |
| **Hamartia** | Tragic flaw / error of the hero |
| **Hubris** | Excessive pride |
| **Deus ex machina** | Sudden artificial solution to a plot |
| **Soliloquy** | Character speaks thoughts alone on stage ("To be, or not to be") |
| **Aside** | Short remark heard by the audience, not by other characters |
| **Poetic justice** | Virtue rewarded, vice punished (term by **Thomas Rymer**) |
| **Negative capability** | **Keats**'s term: accepting uncertainty without reaching after fact |
| **Objective correlative** | **T. S. Eliot**'s term |
| **Willing suspension of disbelief** | **Coleridge**'s phrase |
| **Bildungsroman** | Novel of growing up (e.g. *David Copperfield*) |
| **Picaresque novel** | Adventures of a roguish hero (*Tom Jones*, *Don Quixote*) |
| **Epistolary novel** | Novel in letters (Richardson's ***Pamela***, 1740) |

## Quick revision
- ***The Pilgrim's Progress*** = religious allegory by **Bunyan**; hero **Christian**.
- ***Gulliver's Travels*** (1726) = satire by **Swift**; Lilliput, Brobdingnag, Houyhnhnms, Yahoos.
- **Like/as** → simile; **no like/as** → metaphor.
- "The pen is mightier than the sword" → **metonymy**.
- "Bitter-sweet", "open secret" → **oxymoron**.
- "The Child is father of the Man" → **paradox**.
- "Death lays his icy hand on kings" → **personification**.
- "I came, I saw, I conquered" → **climax**.

## Practice MCQ
**1.** "My love is like a red, red rose" contains:
(a) a metaphor (b) a simile (c) personification (d) an oxymoron

**2.** *The Pilgrim's Progress* is:
(a) a satire (b) a mock epic (c) an allegory (d) a picaresque novel

**3.** "The pen is mightier than the sword" is an example of:
(a) metonymy (b) hyperbole (c) simile (d) alliteration

**4.** Who wrote *Gulliver's Travels*?
(a) Daniel Defoe (b) Alexander Pope (c) John Dryden (d) Jonathan Swift

**5.** "Open secret" is an example of:
(a) paradox (b) euphemism (c) oxymoron (d) litotes

**6.** "The Child is father of the Man" is an example of:
(a) paradox (b) simile (c) apostrophe (d) climax

**7.** "Death lays his icy hand on kings" contains:
(a) alliteration (b) personification (c) pun (d) antithesis

**8.** The Houyhnhnms in *Gulliver's Travels* are:
(a) tiny people (b) giants (c) scientists (d) rational horses

## Answer key
| Q | Ans | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | b | Comparison with "like" |
| 2 | c | Bunyan's religious allegory |
| 3 | a | Pen stands for writing, sword for force |
| 4 | d | Swift, 1726 |
| 5 | c | Two opposite words side by side |
| 6 | a | Seemingly absurd statement hiding a truth |
| 7 | b | Death is given a human action |
| 8 | d | The Yahoos are the brutish humans |
