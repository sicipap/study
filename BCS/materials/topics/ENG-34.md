# Literary terms: sonnet, ode, elegy, epic, ballad

> **Why it matters:** Exams ask definitions ("A sonnet has how many lines?"), forms (Petrarchan vs Shakespearean), and famous examples ("*Lycidas* is a…", "*Adonais* is written on…").

## Sonnet 🔥🔥
- A poem of **14 lines**, usually in **iambic pentameter**. From Italian *sonetto* = "little song".
- Perfected by the Italian poet **Petrarch** (poems to **Laura**).
- Introduced into English by **Sir Thomas Wyatt**; the **Earl of Surrey** developed the English form (Surrey also first used **blank verse** in English).
- **Volta** = the "turn" in thought (usually at line 9 in the Petrarchan sonnet).

| Type | Structure | Rhyme scheme | Poets |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Petrarchan / Italian** | **Octave (8) + sestet (6)** | abbaabba + cdecde or cdcdcd | Petrarch, **Milton**, Wordsworth |
| **Shakespearean / English** | **3 quatrains + a couplet** | **abab cdcd efef gg** | Shakespeare |
| **Spenserian** | 3 linked quatrains + couplet | abab bcbc cdcd ee | Spenser (*Amoretti*) |

| Sonnet sequence / sonnet | Poet |
|---|---|
| **154 sonnets** (1609); Sonnet 18 "**Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?**"; "Fair Youth", "Dark Lady" | **Shakespeare** |
| ***Astrophel and Stella*** | **Sir Philip Sidney** |
| ***Amoretti*** | **Edmund Spenser** |
| "**On His Blindness**" ("They also serve who only stand and wait") | **Milton** |
| "**Death, be not proud**" (Holy Sonnets) | **John Donne** |
| "The world is too much with us", "Composed upon Westminster Bridge", "London, 1802" | **Wordsworth** |
| "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer", "Bright star" | **Keats** |
| "**Ozymandias**" | **Shelley** |
| ***Sonnets from the Portuguese*** ("How do I love thee? Let me count the ways") | **Elizabeth Barrett Browning** |

Bangla note: **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** introduced the sonnet (চতুর্দশপদী কবিতা) into Bangla.

## Ode 🔥
- A long, serious **lyric poem in an elevated style**, usually **addressed** to a person, object or idea.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Pindaric** | From Greek poet **Pindar**; strophe, antistrophe, epode |
| **Horatian** | From Latin poet **Horace**; regular stanzas, calm tone |
| **Irregular** | Free stanza lengths (Abraham **Cowley**, Wordsworth's *Immortality Ode*) |

| Ode | Poet | Famous line |
|---|---|---|
| "**Ode to a Nightingale**" (1819) | **Keats** | "My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains"; "Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!" |
| "**Ode on a Grecian Urn**" (1819) | **Keats** | "**Beauty is truth, truth beauty**"; "Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter" |
| "**To Autumn**" (1819) | **Keats** | "Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness" |
| "Ode on Melancholy", "Ode to Psyche" | Keats | |
| "**Ode to the West Wind**" (1819) | **Shelley** | "**If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?**" |
| "**Ode: Intimations of Immortality**" | **Wordsworth** | Epigraph: "The Child is father of the Man" |
| "Dejection: An Ode" | Coleridge | |
| "Ode on a Distant Prospect of Eton College" | **Thomas Gray** | "where ignorance is bliss, / 'Tis folly to be wise" |

## Elegy 🔥
- A poem of **mourning for the dead** (or a serious meditation on death).
- **Pastoral elegy:** the poet and the dead person appear as shepherds.

| Elegy | Poet | Mourns |
|---|---|---|
| ***Lycidas*** (1637) 🔥 | **Milton** | His college friend **Edward King** (drowned) |
| ***Adonais*** (1821) 🔥 | **Shelley** | **John Keats** |
| ***Thyrsis*** | **Matthew Arnold** | Poet **Arthur Hugh Clough** |
| ***In Memoriam A.H.H.*** (1850) 🔥 | **Tennyson** | Friend **Arthur Henry Hallam**; "'Tis better to have loved and lost / Than never to have loved at all"; "Nature, red in tooth and claw" |
| "**Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard**" (1751) 🔥 | **Thomas Gray** | Humble villagers; "The paths of glory lead but to the grave"; "Full many a flower is born to blush unseen" |
| "**O Captain! My Captain!**" | **Walt Whitman** | **Abraham Lincoln** |
| "In Memory of W. B. Yeats" | W. H. Auden | Yeats |
| "Do not go gentle into that good night" | Dylan Thomas | His dying father (a **villanelle**) |

## Epic 🔥
- A **long narrative poem** on heroic deeds, in an **elevated style**, with a hero of national importance, supernatural forces, an **invocation to the Muse**, starting ***in medias res*** ("in the middle of things"), and **epic (Homeric) similes**.
- **Primary / folk epic:** grows from oral tradition (*Iliad*, *Beowulf*). **Secondary / literary epic:** written by one poet in imitation (*Aeneid*, *Paradise Lost*).

| Epic | Poet / origin |
|---|---|
| ***Iliad*** (Trojan War, Achilles), ***Odyssey*** (Odysseus) | **Homer** (Greek) |
| ***Aeneid*** (Aeneas) | **Virgil** (Latin) |
| ***Beowulf*** | Anonymous; **oldest epic in English** (Old English) |
| ***The Divine Comedy*** | **Dante** (Italian) |
| ***The Faerie Queene*** (unfinished; for Elizabeth I) | **Edmund Spenser** |
| ***Paradise Lost*** (1667; 12 books in the 1674 edition; blank verse; "justify the ways of God to men") 🔥 | **John Milton** |
| ***Paradise Regained*** (1671) | Milton |
| ***Ramayana*** / ***Mahabharata*** | **Valmiki** / **Vyasa** |
| ***Shahnameh*** | **Ferdowsi** (Persian) |
| ***Meghnad Badh Kabya*** (1861) | **Michael Madhusudan Dutt** (Bangla; blank verse) |

**Mock epic / mock-heroic:** uses the grand epic style for a trivial subject. ***The Rape of the Lock*** by **Alexander Pope** (theft of a lock of **Belinda**'s hair by the Baron); Dryden's *Mac Flecknoe*; Pope's *The Dunciad*.

## Ballad 🔥
- A short **narrative song** passed down orally; simple language, dialogue, **refrain**, and the **ballad stanza** (quatrain, rhyme **abcb**, lines of 4 and 3 stresses).
- **Folk ballads:** "Sir Patrick Spens", "Chevy Chase", "Lord Randal", the Robin Hood ballads. Collected in Thomas **Percy**'s *Reliques of Ancient English Poetry* (1765).
- **Literary ballads:** Coleridge's "**The Rime of the Ancient Mariner**" (in ***Lyrical Ballads***, 1798, with Wordsworth; the albatross; "Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink"); Keats's "**La Belle Dame sans Merci**"; Wilde's "The Ballad of Reading Gaol".

## Other verse forms (quick table)
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Lyric** | Short poem expressing personal feeling |
| **Blank verse** | **Unrhymed iambic pentameter** (Milton, Shakespeare) |
| **Free verse** | No fixed metre or rhyme (Whitman) |
| **Couplet** / **heroic couplet** | Two rhyming lines / rhymed iambic pentameter pairs (Pope, Dryden) |
| **Quatrain / sestet / octave** | Stanza of 4 / 6 / 8 lines |
| **Terza rima** | aba bcb cdc… (Dante) |
| **Rhyme royal** | 7 lines, ababbcc (Chaucer) |
| **Spenserian stanza** | 9 lines, last one an alexandrine, ababbcbcc |
| **Villanelle** | 19 lines, two refrains |
| **Limerick** | 5 lines, aabba, humorous |
| **Haiku** | Japanese, 3 lines, 5-7-5 syllables |
| **Dramatic monologue** | One speaker to a silent listener (Browning's "**My Last Duchess**") |
| **Epitaph** | Lines written on a tomb |

## Quick revision
- Sonnet = **14 lines**; Petrarchan = **octave + sestet**; Shakespearean = **3 quatrains + couplet (abab cdcd efef gg)**.
- Sonnet introduced into English by **Wyatt**.
- *Lycidas* → **Edward King**; *Adonais* → **Keats**; *In Memoriam* → **Hallam**; *Thyrsis* → **Clough**.
- "Ode to the West Wind" = **Shelley**; "Ode to a Nightingale", "Grecian Urn" = **Keats**.
- Oldest English epic: ***Beowulf***; *Paradise Lost* = **Milton**; mock epic *The Rape of the Lock* = **Pope**.
- Ballad stanza rhyme: **abcb**; *Lyrical Ballads* (1798) = **Wordsworth & Coleridge**.
